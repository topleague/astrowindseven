Web hosting is the service that stores your website's files on a computer, called a server, that is always connected to the internet. When someone types your domain name, the server sends them your pages. Without hosting, a domain is only an address with nothing behind it.

This is part 2 of our beginner series. If you have not yet registered a name, start with part 1, [how to buy a domain name](/how-to-buy-a-domain-name/). Part 3 covers [choosing a CMS](/cms-wordpress-joomla-drupal/).

Hosting plans look alike on a sales page, but they differ in ways that matter once your site is live. Here is what to look at.

## The main types of hosting

- **Shared hosting.** Many sites share one server. It is the cheapest option and suits a small business site or blog with modest traffic. The downside is that a busy neighbour on the same server can slow you down.
- **Managed WordPress hosting.** A shared or cloud plan tuned for WordPress, often with automatic updates, caching, backups and security. It costs more than basic shared hosting but takes work off your hands.
- **VPS or cloud hosting.** You get a guaranteed slice of a server's resources. It is a step up for busy sites, online shops and custom applications, and often needs more technical care unless it is managed for you.
- **Dedicated server.** A whole server for one customer. It is rarely needed by a small business.
- **Static or edge hosting.** For sites built as plain files rather than a database-driven CMS, some platforms host them very cheaply and quickly. Worth knowing about, but most beginners will use one of the types above.

A small business brochure site with a few dozen pages usually does very well on good shared or managed hosting. Move up only when you have a reason, such as slowness under traffic or an online store with many orders.

## What to check before you sign up

### Price and renewal price

Like domains, hosting is often sold at a low first-term price that rises at renewal. Compare the renewal price and the length of the term the discount needs you to commit to. Cheapest is rarely best: a few hundred rupees saved a month is not worth hours of downtime.

### Support

When something breaks, support is what you are paying for. Find out which channels exist (chat, phone, email, tickets), whether they are available around the clock, and how people describe the quality of the answers in independent reviews. If you are new, prefer a host with responsive live chat or phone help in your time zone.

### Speed and server resources

You do not need to understand CPUs and RAM, but you should know what your plan limits: storage, monthly visits or resource usage, and the number of sites. Ask which storage type is used; modern SSD or NVMe storage is faster than old disks. Choose a server location near your main audience, or use a host that offers a content delivery network (CDN) to serve pages quickly from several places. Speed affects both visitors and Google's Core Web Vitals measurements.

### Uptime

Many hosts advertise a very high uptime figure. Read what is actually promised and what remedy you get if they fail. Independent reviews and your own uptime monitoring give a more honest picture.

### Backups

A backup is your protection against mistakes, hacks and failed updates. Look for automatic daily backups that you can restore yourself in a few clicks, with several days of history. Better still, keep an extra copy somewhere other than your host, because a backup stored on the same server can be lost with it. See our [guide to protecting a WordPress website](/protect-your-wordpress-website/) for more on this.

### Security and SSL

A site needs HTTPS, and most good hosts include a free SSL certificate and install it for you. Also check whether the host offers malware scanning, firewalls and regular software updates.

### Email

You can use a professional address such as name@yourbusiness.com. Some hosts include mailboxes in the plan; others expect you to use a separate email service. Either is fine, but check spam filtering, mailbox size and whether the email service is dependable, because lost email costs real customers. Do not rely on your site sending its own contact-form messages without testing them.

### Control panel and ease of use

A control panel is the dashboard where you manage domains, email, files and one-click installs. cPanel and Plesk are well known, and many hosts now offer their own simpler panels. Choose one you can use without phoning support for every small change. A one-click WordPress installer is useful for beginners.

### Room to grow

Check how easy it is to move to a bigger plan when you need one, and whether this means moving servers or a short outage. If you expect a large seasonal spike, such as a festival sale, ask how the host handles traffic surges, because an out-of-stock page costs less than a site that is down.

### Technology versions

A good host keeps current, supported versions of PHP and the database, and lets you choose among them. Outdated server software is a security risk and slows modern WordPress.

## Plan your exit

Sooner or later you may change hosts. Before you commit, check how easy it is to leave. Can you export your files and database yourself? Does the host charge for a migration or lock you into a long term? Is your domain registered separately, so you can point it to a new server? Keeping the domain account in your own name, as suggested in part 1, makes any move far simpler.

## Quick checklist

- Renewal price is acceptable.
- Support works at hours you need.
- Daily backups, restorable by you.
- Free SSL and sensible security.
- A server location close to your visitors, or a CDN.
- Room to upgrade without drama.
- Easy to leave.
