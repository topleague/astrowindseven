Choosing an SEO company is difficult because the work is hard to judge in advance. You are asked to pay for something whose results appear months later, and you may not know enough about the subject to tell a skilled provider from a confident one. We run an SEO company ourselves, so we are not neutral, but we have been on the hiring side of many conversations and have seen what poor SEO does to a business.

This guide is for owners and managers in Odisha, elsewhere in India or abroad. The same questions apply anywhere. You are not testing whether they know all the jargon. You are testing whether they will be honest, competent and clear about what they will do for you.

## Start with what you want

Before you speak to anyone, decide what the website needs to do. More phone calls from your city? Online orders? Leads from customers abroad? An SEO company can only plan well if the goal is concrete. It also lets you judge their proposal: does it address your goal, or is it a generic package?

## Questions to ask, and what good answers sound like

### "What will you do for us, and why?"

A competent provider will describe a plan specific to your site: a technical review, research into what your customers search for, improvements to key pages, new or better content, local search work if relevant, and links earned through useful content and real relationships. They will explain why each step matters. If you hear only "we will optimize your site and build backlinks", ask for detail.

### "How do you do keyword research?"

You should hear about understanding customers' needs and the intent behind searches, not just picking terms with high volume. Ask how they decide which pages should target which topics. Our [keyword research guide](/keyword-research/) shows what a sound approach looks like.

### "How do you build links?"

This question reveals more than any other. Directory submissions, article-directory posts, bought links, link exchanges and private blog networks were once common and are now risky at best. Good answers focus on creating content and resources worth linking to, local citations and listings that are genuine and consistent, and outreach to relevant sites. If the answer is vague or secretive, treat it as a warning.

### "How will we measure progress?"

Good providers agree on measures that matter to you: leads, enquiries, orders, calls and visibility for important searches, not just rankings or traffic. Ask for a sample report. It should be readable and should explain what was done, what happened and what comes next.

### "Who will do the work, and who is my contact?"

Find out who is actually working on your site and whether it is outsourced. Ask to speak to the person who will manage your account. Clear communication, including the ability to write a plain email and explain things without jargon, is a basic skill. It is also a good sign if they can write sensible page titles and descriptions for your pages.

### "How do you keep up with changes?"

Search changes constantly, including how search results look, how AI features summarise answers, and what Google counts as spam. A good provider can tell you which sources they follow, such as Google's own Search Central documentation and respected industry publications, and give examples of what they changed recently as a result. They should be able to explain in simple terms how search works, what makes a page useful, and how they improve click-through from search results.

### "Can we talk to some current clients?"

Ask for references and look at real examples of their work. Be careful with screenshots of rankings, which can be selective. Ask what the client wanted and what changed.

## Red flags

- **Guaranteed rankings or a "number one on Google" promise.** No one outside Google controls rankings, and Google says this explicitly. Any guarantee is either false or based on easy, meaningless terms.
- **A secret method.** You should understand what is being done to your site.
- **Unusually low prices with big promises.** Skilled work takes time. Cheap packages tend to rely on automation and shortcuts.
- **Unsolicited cold emails claiming your site is "failing".** They are usually templated and not based on a real review of your business.
- **Old tactics.** Mass directory submissions, article-spinning, keyword stuffing and comment spam. If the proposal reads like it was written ten years ago, it probably was.
- **No access for you.** You should be able to see your own Google Search Console, Google Analytics and Google Business Profile. If the company wants to keep these in their own accounts, walk away.
- **Long lock-in contracts without a way out.** Monthly or short terms, with clear deliverables, protect both sides.
- **Pressure.** A rushed decision is a warning in itself.

## Protect yourself in the contract

Agree in writing on the scope of work, deliverables, reporting frequency, price and payment schedule, the length of the agreement and how to end it, who owns the content and accounts (you should), and confidentiality. Insist that you are the owner of the website, domains and analytics accounts, and that the provider has delegated access only.

## Being a good client

SEO works best as a partnership, and a few habits on your side make a real difference:

- Share your business goals and honest numbers, such as which products or services earn the most.
- Describe the project fully when asking for a quote. Providers cannot price accurately without knowing the scope.
- Give feedback and approvals within the agreed time, as delays on your side delay results.
- Pay invoices on time.
- Be realistic. Meaningful results typically take months, and the price should reflect the work required, not a wish.

## What a first conversation should feel like

A good first call involves them asking questions about your business, customers and goals more than they talk about themselves. They should be willing to say "we do not know yet" and "that will not work for your case". If you leave the conversation understanding more than when you started, that is a good sign.
