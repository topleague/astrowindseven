A product description has a harder job than most sales copy. A shopper cannot touch the item, ask a question or meet the seller. The description has to stand in for all of that, in a few lines, and often on a phone screen.

Most descriptions fall into one of two types. Cold ones list features in a flat, impersonal way. Persuasive ones answer the buyer's questions and help them picture the product in use. The difference is rarely clever phrasing. It is mostly research, specificity and respect for the reader. Here is how we approach it.

## Learn the product before you write

You cannot write credibly about something you do not understand. If you have used the product, take notes on what surprised you. If you have not, as is often the case for copywriters, research it properly: the materials, how it is made, who it is for, how it compares with alternatives, and what problems it solves.

Ask the seller for samples, specifications, manufacturer documents and customer feedback. Read reviews of this product and of similar ones. They show what buyers care about, which doubts stop them, and the words they naturally use. Use these insights to shape your description, but never copy reviewers' sentences.

## Answer the questions a buyer would ask

Think like a journalist and cover the facts. For most physical products that means:

- **Size and weight.** Especially important for furniture, appliances, luggage and anything that must fit somewhere.
- **Materials and construction.** They signal quality and durability.
- **Colour, finish and variants.** Say what is available and how they differ.
- **What is in the box.** Include accessories, cables, batteries or manuals, and say what is not included.
- **Care and maintenance.** How to clean, store or charge it.
- **Compatibility.** Models, sizes or systems it works with.
- **Warranty, returns and shipping.** Buyers decide faster when the risk is clear.
- **Who made it.** Brand or manufacturer details can build trust.

Put the facts in a short, scannable list or table, and keep the persuasive writing above it. Accuracy matters more than anything: a wrong measurement or an exaggerated claim creates returns and complaints. For products where safety, health or legal standards apply, state only what you can support, and follow the rules in the markets where you sell.

## Know who you are talking to

A description for a gamer, a new parent and a procurement manager should not sound alike. Decide who is reading and adapt the vocabulary, the level of detail and the tone. Some audiences enjoy humour; others want precise specifications and nothing else. Your customer service queries and reviews will tell you which you have.

## Lead with benefits, back them with features

A feature is what the product has. A benefit is what that means for the buyer. Shoppers care about the second, and believe it because of the first.

- Feature: "Double-walled stainless steel."
- Benefit: "Keeps drinks cold through a long day out."
- Together: "The double-walled stainless steel body keeps cold drinks cold for hours, and the outside stays dry in your bag."

Only claim what is true, and do not give a figure unless you have tested it or the manufacturer states it. Address common doubts too: "Fits a standard cup holder" or "Machine washable at 30 degrees" removes a reason not to buy.

## Prefer concrete words to vague ones

Words such as "premium", "amazing" and "high quality" say very little because every competitor uses them. Replace them with something the reader can picture or verify: the actual fabric, the weight, the capacity, the time it takes, the finish.

Strong verbs also help. "Folds flat in seconds" tells the reader more than "convenient and compact". Use adjectives where they describe something real (colour, texture, size) and avoid stacking them for effect.

## Keep it short and easy to read

Many shoppers read on a phone, so use short sentences and short paragraphs. Break up longer descriptions with a few headings or bullets. Put the key benefit and the key facts near the top, and add detail below for those who want it. A good pattern is a two- or three-sentence summary, then a feature list, then a technical section, such as specifications or care instructions, that readers can open if they need it.

Avoid jargon that your buyer would not use. If a technical term matters, say what it means in plain language the first time.

## Write original text

Copying the manufacturer's description, or another shop's, is one of the most common mistakes in online retail. Your page then reads exactly like many others, gives shoppers no reason to buy from you, and gives search engines little reason to show your page over the original. Write your own text for every product, even if the products are similar. For a large catalogue, begin with your best sellers and work down.

Machine-generated descriptions can speed up the first draft, but they also produce bland, repeated wording and sometimes invent specifications. If you use such tools, check every fact against the product data and rewrite until it sounds like your shop.

## Help search engines understand the page

Use the product's name, type and key attributes naturally in the title, the opening text and the headings, the way a shopper would describe it. Structured data (Product markup) can describe price, availability and reviews in a form search engines understand; our post on [schema markup best practices](/schema-markup-best-practices/) explains how to add it correctly. Good images with descriptive alt text matter too, and the details should match what is shown on the page.

## A checklist before you publish

1. Does the description say who the product is for?
2. Are size, material, colour, contents, care and warranty all stated?
3. Does each important feature have a benefit next to it?
4. Is every claim true and something we can support?
5. Is the text original and easy to read on a phone?
6. Is there a clear next step: add to basket, choose size, see delivery details?

## In short

Know the product, answer the buyer's questions, show what the features mean in use, and write your own words. Perfecting every description at once is not realistic, but improving the pages that bring the most traffic and sales usually pays for itself.
