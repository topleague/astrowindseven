Visitors decide very quickly whether a page is for them. If your headline sounds like every competitor and your copy is full of words nobody believes, they leave. Good web copy does the opposite: it is specific, it speaks to a real problem and it makes the next step clear.

This guide covers the clichés to cut, how to write headlines, and how to structure a sales page so that more of your visitors take action.

## Clichés that weaken your copy

Some words have been used so often that readers no longer register them. Others make claims you cannot prove in a sentence. Watch out for:

- **Buzzwords:** "world-class", "state-of-the-art", "cutting-edge", "innovative", "pioneering", "breakthrough", "industry-standard", "best-in-class". If it is true, show it. "Innovative" tells us nothing, but "cuts invoice processing from two days to two hours" tells us something (provided it is true for you).
- **Superlatives:** "amazing", "stunning", "ultimate", "fantastic". These carry weight when customers say them about you, and little when you say them about yourself.
- **Loose promises:** "fast delivery", "top quality", "excellent customer service". Replace them with facts. How many days? Which material? How quickly do you reply?
- **Jargon:** "leverage", "synergy", "end-to-end solutions", "robust". Use words your customer would use. "Use" beats "leverage".

A useful test: could a competitor paste the sentence onto their own site without changing anything? If yes, it is not saying much. Replace it with something only you can say.

### Turn vague claims into specifics

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| Fast delivery | Delivered in 2 to 4 working days |
| Top quality material | Made from 100% cotton, pre-washed |
| Excellent support | We reply to every email within one working day |
| Affordable pricing | Plans start at a clear, published rate |

Only publish a specific claim that is accurate and that you can keep. A specific promise you break does more damage than a vague one.

## Writing headlines that work

The headline decides whether the rest gets read. It has to promise something relevant to the right person. A few reliable styles:

- **How-to:** "How to choose a website designer for your clinic". It attracts people who want practical guidance.
- **Question:** "Is your website losing enquiries on mobile?" It works when the first lines of the page answer the question honestly.
- **Why or reasons:** "Why most small business websites do not bring enquiries". This implies an explanation, so the page must deliver one.
- **Direct benefit or command:** "Get a clear quote in 24 hours". Use when you can back it up.
- **Warning or "don't":** "Don't sign an SEO contract before you ask these questions". It prompts action, but avoid scare tactics.

Whatever the style, the rules are the same:

1. Be clear before being clever. A reader should understand the headline without extra context.
2. Include something specific: a number, an outcome, a group of people or a problem.
3. Make a promise the page keeps. Misleading headlines lose trust and increase people leaving at once.
4. Write several options and choose the best. Two or three minutes of drafting makes a big difference.
5. Mention the main search phrase naturally if the page is meant to rank, but keep it readable.

## Writing a sales page, step by step

A sales page is not a brochure. It has a job: help a particular person decide to contact or buy. A good order:

### 1. Open with the reader's situation

Start with their problem, not your history. Think about what brought them here. Are they frustrated with a vendor that did not communicate? Worried about cost? Short of time? Showing that you understand their situation earns the next paragraph.

### 2. State what you offer and what makes it different

Before writing, list the points that set you apart: your process, speed, guarantees, experience, pricing approach or reporting. Put at least one in the headline or first paragraph. Customers are often not only comparing price. Many come to you because the last provider was unreliable or unclear.

### 3. Explain the benefits briefly

Say what the customer gets, in plain words. Use short bullet points, and keep each to a sentence or two. Features describe the product, benefits describe what changes for the customer. Cover both, with the benefit first.

### 4. Make the page easy to scan

Most people skim, often on a phone. Use short sentences and paragraphs, clear subheadings that carry a benefit, and lists where they help. Bold the key phrases, not whole paragraphs. Avoid dense blocks.

### 5. Add proof

Claims become believable with evidence: real customer reviews with permission, examples of your work, certifications, photos of your team, clear policies. Do not invent testimonials or numbers. Honest, modest proof beats big claims.

### 6. Answer objections

Think about what stops someone from contacting you: price, timing, risk, whether you suit their size of business. Address these directly, perhaps in a short FAQ.

### 7. End with a clear call to action

Tell the reader exactly what to do next, and make it easy: "Request a quote", "Call us on a working day", "Send us your requirements". One main action is better than five. Offer a simple form and, where it suits your customers, a phone or WhatsApp option. Keep forms short; every extra field loses some people. Make sure the action button is visible on mobile.

## Tone: simple and human

Write the way a helpful person speaks. Use everyday words, short sentences and "you". Be friendly without forcing slang or humour that might not translate for readers in India, the USA and beyond. Clear language is also kinder to readers who use English as a second language.

Good copy has more in common with a conversation than with an essay. Read it aloud. If you stumble, simplify.

## Edit before publishing

- Cut every sentence that does not move the reader forward.
- Replace any cliché or vague claim from the list above.
- Check that each claim is accurate and that you can deliver it.
- Look at the page on a phone.
- Proofread for spelling and grammar. Mistakes signal carelessness.
- Test: change the headline or button text later and compare results if you have enough traffic.
