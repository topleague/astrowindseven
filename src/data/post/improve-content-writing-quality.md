Most web content fails for a simple reason: it was written to fill a page, not to help a reader. Search engines and readers have both become better at noticing the difference. Google's own guidance asks for content that is helpful and created for people first, and that guidance has not changed in spirit for years.

This post sets out the process we follow when writing for clients. It works whether you are writing your own blog, briefing a writer, or editing someone else's draft. It is deliberately practical: each step is something you can do this week.

## 1. Write for your audience, not for yourself

Writing about what you find fascinating is fine as a hobby. For a business site, start from what your customers want to know. Talk to your sales and support teams, read customer emails and reviews, and spend time in the forums, groups and communities where your audience asks questions. The wording people use there is often better than any keyword tool will show.

Then check demand: search for the topic, look at the suggestions Google offers, and use a keyword research method to see whether people actually look for it. Our [keyword research guide](/keyword-research/) explains how. Be realistic about competition. A new site is unlikely to beat big established ones on broad topics, so start with narrower, more specific questions that you can answer better.

## 2. Match the search intent

People searching have a goal: to learn something, compare options, find a place, or buy. Content that fits that goal tends to perform; content that ignores it does not, however well it is written.

A practical test is to read the top results for your target query and ask:

- What do they have in common? Guides, lists, product pages, videos?
- What do they cover that you would also need to cover?
- What do they miss, get wrong, or explain badly?

Your aim is not to copy them, but to produce the most useful answer: more accurate, clearer, more complete, better illustrated, or based on real experience they lack. Length is a result of that, not a target. A long article that wanders is worse than a short one that answers the question.

## 3. Build the outline first

Before writing a few thousand words, write the outline. List the headings, the key points under each, and the order. An outline lets you see gaps and repetition early, and gives you somewhere to return to when you get stuck.

Use competing pages to check you have not forgotten an important subtopic, but organize the piece around your reader's questions, in the order they would ask them. Each heading should tell the reader what they will get below it.

## 4. Research properly and keep your sources

Good content rests on facts you can stand behind. For each piece:

- Use primary or authoritative sources: official documentation, government or industry bodies, original studies, and your own data.
- Note where each fact came from, so you can check or update it later. A simple spreadsheet or notes app works.
- Test things yourself where you can. If you are writing about how to do something, do it and take notes and photos. First-hand experience is what separates useful content from rewritten summaries.
- Cite sources in the text where it helps the reader, and link to them when they are trustworthy.
- Keep your own collection of useful finds (articles, statistics, screenshots, customer questions) in one place. It becomes a head start for future posts.

Google's quality guidelines describe the qualities it wants to reward as experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness, often shortened to E-E-A-T. You cannot add these with a trick. You show them by using real experience, naming the author, explaining their background and being accurate and open about limits.

## 5. Write clearly

Clear writing is the main quality signal readers notice.

- **Lead with the point.** Put the answer or promise in the first paragraph. Do not make readers wade through a history of the topic.
- **Use short sentences and short paragraphs.** Many readers are on phones. A wall of text is the quickest way to lose them.
- **Choose plain words.** Jargon is a private language of your industry. If your reader is not in that industry, explain the term or use a simpler one. Tools such as readability checkers can flag long sentences, but your own ear is better.
- **Keep a natural tone.** Formal does not mean credible. Write as you would explain the topic to a customer across a desk, using connecting phrases such as "so", "but", "for example" and "that means" to carry the reader from one idea to the next.
- **Be specific.** "Reduces setup time" says less than "most customers finish setup in one afternoon" (if that is true). Avoid padding and the vague, overheated language we cover in [copywriting clichés](/copywriting-cliches-kill-your-conversion-rate/).
- **Use visuals where they help.** Screenshots, diagrams, photos and short videos should explain something, not decorate.

## 6. Headlines and openings

A headline has two jobs: to tell readers and search engines what the page is about, and to make the right person want to click. Be clear first and clever second. A promise the page cannot keep may win a click, but it loses the reader straight away.

The opening should show the reader they are in the right place and what they will gain. A short example, a direct question or a plain statement of the problem usually works better than a long preamble.

## 7. Write how-to content with care

How-to articles are among the most useful and most searched types of content. To make them work:

- Pick topics you can genuinely explain, and cover them from the beginner's point of view.
- Number the steps and put one action in each.
- Say what the reader needs beforehand and what they should see at each stage.
- Show the result of important steps with an image or short clip.
- Be careful with sensitive subjects. For health, legal, financial or safety topics, only publish what is accurate and reviewed by someone qualified, and say plainly where the reader should seek a professional.
- Respect copyright. Do not use other people's images or text without permission, and credit sources.

## 8. Make it worth linking to

If you want other sites to mention your work, give them a reason: original data from your own business, a clear explanation, a useful template, a well-made diagram, or a carefully reviewed list of resources. Links are earned by being the best available reference on something, not requested in bulk. See our note on [internal linking](/internal-linking-anchor-text/) for the part you control, on your own site.

## 9. Edit hard, then have someone else read it

Put the draft away for a day if you can, then edit. Cut repetition, vague sentences and anything that does not serve the reader. Read it aloud to catch awkward phrasing, and check names, numbers, dates and links.

Spelling and grammar tools help with surface errors. They do not tell you whether the argument holds or the order makes sense, so ask a colleague or friend who matches your reader to read the piece and tell you where they got lost. If you use AI tools for drafting or editing, treat the output as unchecked: verify every fact, remove generic filler, and add what only you know. Publishing mass-produced, unoriginal text gives readers nothing and risks being treated by search engines as low-value content.

## 10. End well and keep it fresh

Finish with something useful: a short summary, the next step, or a pointer to related material. Do not stop abruptly or tack on a generic "share this" line.

Then maintain it. Revisit important pages at least once a year to correct outdated information, replace broken links and add what you have since learned. Content that stays accurate is worth more each year; content that is left alone slowly stops being trusted.

## A short checklist

Before you publish, ask:

1. Does it answer a real question for a defined reader?
2. Does it match what searchers expect to find?
3. Is every fact accurate and sourced?
4. Does it include something only we could add, such as experience, data or examples?
5. Is it easy to scan and read on a phone?
6. Has someone other than the author read it?

Quality takes planning and time, and there is no shortcut that works for long. A steady habit of doing these things will do more for your site than any single tactic.
