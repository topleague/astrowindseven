For many small business sites, the About page is one of the most visited pages after the home page. People who are nearly ready to contact you click it to decide whether you are real, reliable and right for them. Yet most About pages are a paragraph of company history that nobody reads.

A good About page is not about you in the way you might expect. It is about you in the way a customer needs: who stands behind this business, and why should I trust them with my problem?

## Two myths to drop

**"It should be about us."** It should be about the reader's problem, with your story as the evidence that you can solve it. Tell the parts of your story that matter to a customer: why you started, what you have learned, what you do differently.

**"Minimal design is always best."** It depends on what you sell. A writer or consultant can let plain words carry the page. A design studio or restaurant should show its work and atmosphere. Match the page to what your customers will judge you on.

## What to include

### A headline that says something

"About Us" tells nobody anything. Keep it if the navigation needs it, but put a clear headline above the content, such as what you do and for whom. "We build and maintain websites for small businesses in India and the USA" is better than a slogan.

### Who you help and what changes for them

Open with the customer's situation. Then say how you help and what working with you is like. Use plain verbs. "We help" is stronger than "we endeavour to assist". Skip the empty adjectives and the claims you cannot back up. Our piece on [copywriting clichés that kill conversions](/copywriting-cliches-kill-your-conversion-rate/) lists the worst offenders.

### Your story, told honestly

When and why the business began, who runs it, and what has shaped how you work. Real specifics (founding year, location, the kind of clients you serve) convince better than superlatives. Do not exaggerate. Readers notice, and credibility is hard to win back.

### Real people

Photos of the founder and the team, with names and roles, make a business feel accountable. A recent, genuine photo beats a stock image. A short video can also work, if it is well made and short.

### Proof

Use testimonials, client names you have permission to show, certifications, years in business, awards, or a sample of your work. Everything must be real. Fake or invented reviews can damage your reputation and may break consumer protection rules in some markets. A named testimonial with a specific outcome beats several vague praises.

### Answers to unspoken doubts

Think of what a cautious visitor wonders: How big are you? Who will I deal with? What if it does not work out? How much does it cost? How long does it take? You do not need to answer every point, but address the ones that stop people from enquiring.

### One clear next step

End with an action that suits a reader who has just learned about you: contact, request a quote, book a call, or see your work. Offering a small helpful extra, such as a checklist or a free consultation, can work if it is genuinely valuable. One main action is better than five.

## Layout and placement

- Put it in the main navigation, usually beside Home, so it is easy to find.
- Put the key message at the top. Many visitors will skim the first screen and decide whether to continue.
- Use short paragraphs and subheadings. People scan.
- Make sure it works well on a phone, where most of your visitors may be.
- Use clear contact details and, where relevant, your address and business registration information. For local businesses, keep details consistent with your Google Business Profile.

## A simple structure to start from

1. Headline that states what you do and for whom
2. Short opening about the problem you solve
3. Your story and what you stand for
4. Team photos and names
5. Proof: reviews, results, credentials
6. Common concerns answered
7. Call to action

## Edit it like a customer

Read the draft and cross out anything the customer would not care about. Replace "we are passionate about" with something someone could check. Then ask one person outside your business to read it and tell you what you do and who you do it for. If they cannot, rewrite the headline.

Revisit the page once a year. Teams change, clients change, and a stale About page quietly signals a neglected business.
