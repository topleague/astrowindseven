Internal links are the links from one page of your website to another. They are one of the few SEO levers fully under your control, and they cost nothing. Yet most small business sites either ignore them or link in awkward, repetitive ways.

This guide explains what internal links do, how to choose the clickable words (anchor text), and how to think about nofollow, a topic that confuses many site owners.

## What internal links do

Internal links serve two readers at once.

**Visitors** use them to move to the next useful page. A good link answers the question "where do I go to learn more?" at exactly the moment it occurs to the reader.

**Search engines** use them to discover pages, understand how your content is organised, and judge which pages matter most. A page that nothing links to is hard for crawlers to find. A page linked from many relevant places signals that you consider it important. Google's own documentation recommends using links that are crawlable and descriptive, which you can read at developers.google.com/search.

Internal links do not need tricks. If a link helps a person, it will generally help search engines too.

## Choosing anchor text

Anchor text is the visible, clickable wording of a link. Good anchor text tells the reader, and the crawler, what they will find on the other side.

### Write it for people

Use a short natural phrase from the sentence you are already writing. Compare:

- Weak: "To learn more, click here."
- Better: "We explain how to choose search terms in our [keyword research guide](/keyword-research/)."

"Click here" and "read more" tell nobody anything. Descriptive wording also helps people who use screen readers, who often navigate by listing the links on a page.

### Do not force exact phrases

Older advice told people to use the exact target keyword as anchor text every time. Repeating the same exact phrase across dozens of links looks artificial and gains you nothing. Vary the wording naturally, and let the sentence decide. If the link makes sense when read aloud, it is fine.

### A practical method

Here is how we add links when we edit a draft:

1. Read the draft as a beginner would and mark every term or idea that deserves a fuller explanation.
2. Note phrases similar to what people might type into a search box. These often make good topics for new posts too.
3. Search your own site for each of these ideas and see which existing page covers it best.
4. Link the most natural phrase in the sentence to that page. Pick one strong match rather than several weak ones.
5. If no page exists, add the topic to your list of future posts.

This also works in reverse. When you publish a new page, go back to older related pages and add a link to it. New content otherwise stays hidden from the rest of the site.

## Link where it is useful

### Go deep, not just to the top

Many sites link almost entirely to the homepage and contact page. Those pages are already in your main menu, so repeating the link adds little. The real value is in linking to useful pages sitting deeper in the site: guides, service details, product categories and older posts that still deserve readers.

That said, a link to a relevant service or contact page is perfectly sensible when the reader is ready to act. A post on website speed can naturally lead to your web development page. Just do not bolt the same link onto every paragraph.

### Do not overdo it

You may have read that Google stops after 100 or 150 links on a page. That was old guidance, and Google has since said there is no fixed limit in this sense. The practical limit is the reader's patience. Remember that menus, footers and sidebars already contain links, so think about how many sit in the body text. If links crowd every sentence, none of them stand out.

A sensible approach is to add a link wherever it truly helps, and nowhere else.

### Build clear groups of related pages

For a bigger topic, create one main page that gives the overview and then links to detailed pages on each part. Each detailed page links back to the main one and to its neighbours. This hub-and-spoke pattern makes the structure obvious to both visitors and crawlers. Our [content marketing guide](/content-marketing-guide/) works this way.

### Keep important pages within a few clicks

Aim for every important page to be reachable within about three clicks of the homepage. Use breadcrumbs where they suit the site, and check for orphan pages that have no links pointing to them. SEO crawling tools and Google Search Console can help you find them.

### Fix broken links

Links to deleted or moved pages frustrate visitors and waste crawling effort. Check periodically, and redirect or update links when a page changes address.

## What about nofollow?

A nofollow tag is an attribute added to a link to tell search engines not to treat it as an endorsement. You will mostly meet it on external links.

The old story was that links are either "followed" (valuable) or "nofollow" (worthless), and that nofollow links could be used to shape how authority flowed through a site. Those ideas are dated. Today:

- Google describes `rel="nofollow"` as a hint, not a strict command. It also offers `rel="sponsored"` for paid links and `rel="ugc"` for user-generated content such as comments and forum posts.
- You should use these attributes for what they are meant for: paid placements, affiliate links and links inside user-submitted content.
- **Do not put nofollow on your own internal links** to try to steer authority. It does not work the way people once hoped, and it can only make your own pages harder to discover.
- Do not obsess over whether a link someone gives you is followed. A mention on a relevant, respected site can send real visitors and build awareness whatever the attribute says. Focus on the quality and relevance of where links come from.

## A short checklist

- Link to the best matching page, using descriptive, natural anchor text.
- Link from older pages to new ones, as well as the reverse.
- Do not repeat the same exact-match phrase everywhere.
- Avoid "click here" and bare URLs.
- Make sure every important page has at least a few relevant links pointing to it.
- Check for broken links regularly.
- Leave internal links as normal links.
