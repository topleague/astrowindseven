Hiring a content writer looks simple: find someone who writes well, agree a price, and wait for the drafts. In practice, many businesses go through two or three writers before finding one who suits them, and lose months and money along the way. Most of that waste can be avoided by hiring more carefully the first time.

We have worked with many writers over the years and have seen both sides of the process. This guide covers what to look for, how to test it, what the usual warning signs are, and how to end a relationship that is not working.

## Start with your own brief

Before contacting anyone, write down what you need: the type of content (blog posts, service pages, product descriptions, emails), the audience, the volume per month, the tone, and what success looks like. A writer cannot guess these, and vague briefs produce vague work. A good brief also helps you compare quotes fairly, because every candidate is pricing the same job.

## What to check in a writer

### Relevant experience

Experience in your industry helps, because the writer will need less explaining. But it is not essential if the writer can research well and ask good questions. What you want is evidence of both: a record of writing for businesses like yours, or a clear method for learning a subject quickly.

### Samples you can verify

Ask for published samples with links, not files in a Word document. A real portfolio lets you see how the pieces look live, and in some cases lets you check the byline. Read them as a customer would. Are they clear, accurate and easy to follow? Do the pieces sound alike, or can the writer change tone to suit the client?

Run a few paragraphs of the samples through a plagiarism checker to confirm they are original. Be aware that AI-detection tools are unreliable and should not be used as proof of anything. Ask the writer directly instead (see below).

### A paid test piece

Whatever the CV says, ask for a short test on a topic from your own business. Pay for it at a fair rate; good writers will not work for free, and an unpaid test mainly filters out the experienced ones. A piece of a few hundred words is enough to judge accuracy, structure, tone and how well the writer follows a brief. Notice also how they handle the process: did they ask clarifying questions, or just send something back?

### References and testimonials

Ask for one or two past clients you can contact. A testimonial on a website is easy to fake; a conversation with a real client is not. Ask about reliability, how the writer handles feedback, and whether the client would hire them again.

### Online presence

You would not hire a web designer with no website, and the same applies here. A writer or firm does not need a large site, but a basic website, a professional profile or a small blog shows that they are real, accountable and active. Be wary of anyone who claims to be an established agency and cannot show a business address, a team page or a working site.

## Ask about AI use

In 2026, you should ask every writer and agency how they use AI tools. Many writers use them for outlines, research prompts or editing, and that can be fine. What matters is that someone accountable checks the facts, adds real expertise, and that the final piece is not generic text pasted from a tool. Agree on this up front, in writing, including whether you expect disclosure. Our post on [improving content quality](/improve-content-writing-quality/) explains what good looks like.

## Pricing: what to expect and how to compare

Writers charge in different ways: per word, per article, per hour, or on a monthly retainer. Per-word pricing is easy to compare but can reward padding. Per-piece pricing is simpler when the scope is clear. Retainers suit ongoing work.

Get quotes from several writers and compare what is included: research, revisions, SEO guidance, images, formatting and uploads. Do not choose on price alone. Very low rates usually mean little research, recycled text or an overworked writer, and the cost then reappears as your time spent rewriting. Equally, a high price does not guarantee quality, which is why the test piece matters.

## Freelancer or agency?

It is a common belief that freelancers are always cheaper, but that is not necessarily true. An experienced freelancer can charge as much as an agency. The real differences are these:

- **A freelancer** gives you a direct relationship with the person writing, usually with more flexibility. The risk is capacity: if they are ill or overbooked, there is no backup.
- **An agency** can handle volume, cover several subjects, and replace a writer without disrupting you. The risk is inconsistency if the writers change often, and a layer between you and the person doing the work.

If you need a handful of pieces a month, a good freelancer is often ideal. If you need a lot of content across several topics, an agency's structure becomes more useful.

If you go with an agency, ask:

- Will the same writer or team work on my account, and can I approve them?
- Who is my point of contact, and do they understand my business?
- How do you keep quality consistent across writers?
- What happens when a deadline is missed? Some clients write a small discount for significant delays into the contract, which keeps both sides honest.
- Can I speak to one or two current clients?

## Mistakes to avoid

**Choosing only on price or on a bidding marketplace.** Marketplaces are not all bad, and good writers do use them. The problem is that the lowest bids tend to come from people with the least experience, and you are left sorting through many applications. If you use one, shortlist carefully, and apply the same checks as anywhere else.

**Believing inflated claims.** Some individuals present themselves as a large firm with decades of combined experience. Check whether the website, address and team exist, and whether claims are consistent.

**Paying everything in advance.** For a first project, agree on a split: a small deposit, with the rest on delivery or by milestone. Use a payment method or platform that offers some protection if the work is never delivered. Equally, a writer is entitled to reasonable payment terms, so keep to them.

**Skipping the contract.** Even a simple written agreement should cover scope, price, deadlines, revisions, who owns the final text (it should be you, once paid), confidentiality and what happens if either side ends the arrangement.

**Not giving feedback.** Writers improve with specific comments. "Make it better" helps nobody; "Our readers are non-specialists, so explain this term" does.

## Qualities worth looking for

Beyond skill, these traits predict whether a writer is good to work with over the long term:

- **Professional:** honest about capacity, takes on only what they can do well.
- **Reliable:** meets deadlines, or says early when they cannot.
- **Responsive:** answers questions promptly, especially when working remotely.
- **Versatile:** can move from a blog post to a product page or a press release, and keeps learning.
- **Discreet:** respects confidentiality about your business, products and plans.

## When to part ways

You do not have to put up with poor work. Consider ending the relationship when:

- the writing has repeated factual, grammar or spelling errors after feedback
- the content is copied or lightly reworded from other sites
- deadlines are routinely missed without notice
- the writer cannot adapt to your brief or tone
- text appears to be unedited AI output with errors or invented facts

Do it politely, pay for work completed, and collect all files and any access you have given. Then apply the above checks to your next candidate.

## A final thought

Content writing that supports sales is built over time, and the right writer will become part of your team. Spend a week hiring properly, and you will save months of rework.
