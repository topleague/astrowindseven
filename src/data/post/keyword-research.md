Keyword research means finding out which words and phrases your potential customers type into search engines, and deciding which of them your website should target. It remains the starting point of good SEO, because it tells you what to write about, how to name your pages and what your customers are really asking.

It is also where many small businesses go wrong. They pick words they use internally, or chase the most popular terms in their industry, and end up competing with much larger companies for searches that do not even match their offer.

## Start with your customers, not a tool

Before opening any software, answer these questions:

- What do you sell, and to whom?
- What problem does a customer have right before they contact you?
- What words do they use? Not your industry terms, but theirs.
- What questions do they ask on the phone, on WhatsApp or by email?

Your sales team, support inbox and customer reviews are rich sources. If you serve customers in India, remember that people also search in Hinglish and regional languages, and use local place names. If you serve the USA, spelling and vocabulary differ again. Both can matter.

Write a plain list of products, services, problems and questions. This is your seed list.

## Understand search intent

A keyword is only useful if you understand what the searcher wants. Search intent usually falls into four groups:

- **Informational:** "how to register a company in India". The person wants to learn.
- **Navigational:** "your brand name login". The person wants a specific site.
- **Commercial:** "best accounting software for small business". The person is comparing options.
- **Transactional:** "buy", "price", "near me", "hire". The person is ready to act.

Match the page to the intent. A service page should target transactional and commercial phrases. A blog post suits informational ones. If you use a how-to phrase on a sales page, or a sales pitch on a how-to phrase, you will probably rank poorly and the people who do arrive will leave.

The simplest way to check intent is to search the term yourself and look at the first page of results. If Google is showing guides, a guide is what the searcher expects. If it shows product pages or local businesses, a product or local page is needed.

## Where to find keyword ideas

Many of the best sources are free:

- **Google autocomplete and "People also ask".** These show real phrasing and questions. Note them down as you search.
- **Related searches** at the bottom of results pages.
- **Google Search Console.** If your site already has some traffic, this shows the queries people use to find you, and pages that appear in search without earning many clicks. These are some of your easiest opportunities.
- **Google Keyword Planner.** It is part of Google Ads and gives search volume ranges and related terms. It can require an Ads account, and the volume data is more precise when you run campaigns.
- **Google Trends.** Useful for seasonality and for comparing two phrasings.
- **Third-party SEO tools.** Paid tools offer difficulty scores, competitor comparisons and larger keyword databases. They are helpful if you have the budget, but not required to begin.
- **Competitor sites.** Look at the pages and headings of businesses similar to yours. Note what they cover and what they miss.

The older advice to use a particular tool such as the old Google AdWords external tool or Yahoo Overture no longer applies, since those products have changed or disappeared. Choose tools by what you need, not by what was popular years ago.

## Judge each keyword

For each candidate, consider:

1. **Relevance.** Does this match something you actually offer? A popular term that brings the wrong audience is a waste.
2. **Intent.** Does the searcher want what your page provides?
3. **Search volume.** Roughly how many people search for this? Treat tool estimates as guides, not facts.
4. **Competition.** Look at who ranks. If the top results are large brands or major publishers, a small site will find it difficult. If they are thin pages or forums, there may be room.
5. **Value.** Would a visitor from this search be worth having? One enquiry from a specific search can be worth more than a thousand visits from a vague one.

Old advice said to look for "low competition and high volume". Such terms are rare and quickly taken. For small businesses, a better approach is to go narrower.

## Long-tail and local phrases

Long-tail keywords are longer, more specific phrases, such as "WordPress website for dental clinic in Bhubaneswar" rather than "web design". They have lower volume each, but they face less competition and attract people who know what they want. Together they often bring more useful traffic than one broad term.

For businesses serving a local area, add places: city, neighbourhood and region. Make sure your Google Business Profile and your site agree on the name, address and services you offer.

## Organise and use your keywords

Once you have a shortlist, put it into a simple spreadsheet:

- Group similar terms together, because one page can rank for many related phrases.
- Give each group **one main page**. Avoid two pages targeting the same thing, which makes them compete with each other.
- Note the intent and the page type.
- Pick a main phrase and a few supporting phrases for each page.

Then use them where they matter, naturally:

- The page title and main heading
- The opening paragraph
- Subheadings
- The URL
- The meta description, which does not directly affect ranking but influences whether people click
- Image alt text, where it describes the image

Write for people first. Repeating a phrase unnaturally does not help, and search engines are good at understanding related words and meaning. Cover the topic properly and let the wording vary. Our [internal linking guide](/internal-linking-anchor-text/) shows how to connect related pages.

## Revisit your list

Searches change. New terms appear, seasons shift and AI-generated summaries in search results change which questions bring clicks. Review your keywords every few months using Search Console, retire what does not perform and add new topics from real customer questions. If a page ranks but earns few clicks, improve its title and description. If it does not rank, check whether it truly matches what searchers want.

## Common mistakes

- Targeting only the biggest, most competitive terms.
- Choosing words you like rather than words customers use.
- Ignoring intent.
- Putting one phrase on many pages.
- Treating a tool's difficulty score as the truth.
- Researching once and never again.
